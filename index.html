About

I am a PhD candidate at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where I work with Prof. Sarah Millholland. Before coming to MIT, I completed an integrated Bachelor's and Master's (BS-MS) degree at the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research (IISER) Berhampur.

I am interested in a wide range of problems involving both planets and stars. I'm trying to reconstruct how systems became the way we observe them today. A few questions I have been thinking about recently are:

  1. How do planets become the mature worlds we observe today? Mature planets go through billions of years of evolution. I am interested in tracing their histories and understanding which physical processes leave observable imprints on present-day exoplanet populations.
  2. Can planetary systems survive in some of the densest parts of our Galaxy? The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope has made me think more about stars and planets in the Galactic bulge. I am trying to understand how dense stellar environments reshape binaries and planetary systems.
  3. Why are some exoplanets so bizarre? I have a soft spot for the strange planets: highly misaligned, eccentric, and unexpectedly inflated or puffy ones. I like thinking about the dynamical, interior, tidal, and atmospheric evolution that make them so unusual.
  4. Know thy planet, know thy star. We cannot fully understand planets without understanding their host stars. Binary stars offer an enormous playground for dynamics. I like working on both wide binaries that can drive dramatic orbital evolution in planets and close binaries that evolve through tidal synchronization, circularization, and spin-orbit alignment.
  5. What shapes planetary outflows (a closer look)? Planetary outflows are complex. Their structure and evolution depend on stellar irradiation, heating and cooling, ionization, hydrodynamics, and the gravitational and tidal fields in which the planet is embedded. I enjoy using hydrodynamic simulations to study this complexity, and I am especially interested in how outflows feed back on orbital evolution through angular-momentum transport, reaccretion, and torques from asymmetric tails.

Feel free to reach out if you'd like to talk about any of these questions or related topics!

Tracing the evolutionary histories of exoplanetary systems.

Mature planetary systems often carry hidden imprints of the pathways that shaped them. Their orbital architectures—particularly highly misaligned or eccentric orbits—can preserve clues about a dynamically violent past. I use theoretical models to reconstruct these histories and understand the origins of unusual architectures, including the effects of gravitational perturbations from other planets or stellar companions on planetary orbits. I am also interested in how the subsequent tidal evolution of these systems can reshape not only a planet’s orbit, but also its physical properties—for example, tidal heating can inflate a planet’s radius and lower its density.

A complementary question is how the broader planet population evolves with time. Much of what we know about small planets comes from mature systems, giving us primarily the end state after billions of years of evolution. Some of the first occurrence-rate measurements around young stars are now providing snapshots of planetary populations at earlier stages, allowing us to ask how those populations transform with age. I develop computationally efficient models that follow atmospheric mass loss, thermal evolution, and orbital dynamics in a coupled way—an approach that remains relatively unexplored—to understand how these interdependent physical processes sculpt young planets into the mature populations we observe today.

The 3D Hydrodynamics of Planetary Outflows

Planetary outflows are shaped by stellar irradiation, heating and cooling, ionization, hydrodynamics, and the gravitational and tidal fields in which the planet is embedded. I use 3D hydrodynamic simulations to study the structure of these outflows and how they may feed back on orbital evolution through angular-momentum transport, reaccretion, and asymmetric torques.

Planets in a Galactic Context

Planetary systems do not evolve in isolation. I am interested in how the broader Galactic environment—especially dense stellar regions such as the Galactic bulge—can alter binaries and planetary systems through repeated stellar encounters and long-term dynamical evolution.

Tidal Evolution in Binary Stars

Binary stars provide a rich laboratory for studying long-term dynamical evolution. I am interested in how tides drive synchronization, circularization, and spin-orbit evolution in close binaries, and how wider stellar companions can trigger strong dynamical changes in planetary systems.
